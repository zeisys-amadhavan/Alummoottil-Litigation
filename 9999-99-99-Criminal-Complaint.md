#### GROUNDS

*A)* Civil proceedings do not bar prosecution of independently disclosed offences. The complaint concerns entrusted money, non-utilisation, altered Trust records and their subsequent use—not merely beneficiary rights or accounting disputes. *Trisuns Chemical v. Rajesh Agarwal, (1999) 8 SCC 686.*

*B)* This complaint is not intended as leverage in the civil suits. No criminal prayer seeks declaration of beneficiary status, injunction, civil accounts, settlement, withdrawal of defences or any concession in O.S.214/2025 or O.S.243/2024. Criminal responsibility alone is sought. *Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd., (2006) 6 SCC 736.*

*C)* The allegations describe successive alleged acts capable of constituting distinct offences, not merely a civil dispute recast in criminal terminology. Entrusted money, a contemporaneous statement of non-utilisation, later beneficiary-exclusion records, alteration of the original Minutes Book and subsequent production of that record are separately pleaded documentary events. *M. Krishnan v. Vijay Singh, (2001) 8 SCC 645.*

*D)* The documentary sequence supports an inference that A1’s prolonged non-opening of the mandated Trust bank account was deliberate and warrants inquiry into whether it facilitated off-account handling of Trust money. Clause 4 of the founding Deed (p.4) required Trust money to be deposited in a joint account in the Trust’s name. The 22.12.2023 resolution (Minutes Book p.10), signed by A1, expressly resolved to open the Federal Bank account; the 12.01.2024 minutes (p.11), also bearing A1’s signature, recorded that the resolution “has to be followed up” and that the Managing Trustee was “entrusted to speed up this.” Yet the Federal Bank record shows the Trust account was opened only on 28.05.2025, while the same 12.01.2024 minutes record ₹1 crore as having been transferred to a “Construction account.” A1 later swore that during 2007–2024 the Trust had received no funding, donations, income or generated revenue. Taken together, these circumstances are relied upon as circumstantial evidence warranting inquiry into whether A1 intentionally kept Trust-related money outside the mandated Trust banking trail to facilitate dishonest misappropriation, use or conversion; the delay in opening the account is not pleaded by itself as proof of the offence.

*E)* The criminal-breach-of-trust theory is founded upon alleged entrustment for a defined maintenance purpose. The documents identify the money, recipient account, stated maintenance purpose, A2’s contemporaneous assertion of non-utilisation and demand for return, A1’s contemporaneous notice, and the later absence of any produced utilisation/return record. Those facts warrant inquiry into dishonest misappropriation/use/conversion; dishonesty is not inferred merely from deficient accounting. The applicable offence provision must be matched to the date of the alleged criminal act; the pre-2024 correspondence is relied upon for entrustment, purpose and notice, not to apply the BNS retrospectively. *S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar, (2002) 1 SCC 241.*

*F)* Forgery/document offences are distinct from the civil beneficiary dispute. Whether the Complainant is ultimately declared a beneficiary does not determine who altered the Minutes Book or whether an altered record was knowingly used. *M. Krishnan v. Vijay Singh, (2001) 8 SCC 645.*

*G)* Knowing use of a forged document is distinct from personally making the forgery. A1 signed the meeting record and later produced the original containing the alterations. Those acts provide a documentary basis to examine knowledge independently from handwriting authorship; they are not pleaded as proof that A1 personally made the alteration. *A.S. Krishnan v. State of Kerala, (2004) 11 SCC 576.*

*H)* The complaint does not presume authorship of the alterations. Who struck “beneficiary” and inserted “he may claim” remains a matter for evidence. Forgery as maker is alleged only subject to proof linking A1 to the making or alteration. *Sheila Sebastian v. R. Jawaharaj, (2018) 7 SCC 581.*

*I)* Section 215 BNSS is not being circumvented. Court statements are relied upon only as evidence of knowledge, intention and subsequent conduct. Any offence requiring action by the Civil Court is reserved; any independent pre-court forgery, if established, remains separately prosecutable. *Iqbal Singh Marwah v. Meenakshi Marwah, (2005) 4 SCC 370.*

*J)* At the complaint stage, after following the procedure mandated by Section 223 BNSS, the Court examines whether sufficient ground exists to proceed, not whether guilt is finally proved. The complaint identifies specific amounts, dates, documents, statements, signatures and alterations rather than general accusations. Mere attendance at, or signature on, a meeting record is not pleaded as guilt; process is sought only where the materials support that accused’s own act and the required mental element. *Rajesh Bajaj v. State (NCT of Delhi), (1999) 3 SCC 259; Kamal Shivaji Pokarnekar v. State of Maharashtra, (2019) 14 SCC 350.*

*K)* The beneficiary-selection, boundary and RELIS records support inquiry into motive for dishonest dealing with Trust property. The founding Deed defines the beneficiary class as descendants of Alummoottil Chellamma Channathy and contains no one-descendant beneficiary-nomination mechanism. The 22.12.2023 resolution, signed by A1, introduced such a mechanism; later records placed two nominees from A1’s branch in the beneficiary structure and sought to terminate the Complainant’s beneficiary status. Separately, A1/D1’s O.S.243/2024 schedule places A1-owned property on the Trust property’s southern boundary and A1/A6-owned property on its western boundary. Current RELIS records additionally place approximately 40.80 Ares in the individual names of A1 and A6—more than the Trust’s own 38.06 Ares—including Re-Survey 219/20, which the founding Trust Deed itself identifies among properties to be maintained/managed by the Trust. At the Lease Deed’s stated valuation basis of approximately ₹13.20 lakh per Are, those 40.80 Ares correspond to approximately ₹5.39 crore. These circumstances are relied upon as circumstantial evidence of personal conflict and possible motive to concentrate beneficial and physical control over the Trust property, warranting inquiry into whether that motive accompanied dishonest misappropriation, use or conversion; they are not pleaded as direct proof of it.
